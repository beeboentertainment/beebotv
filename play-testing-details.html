Google Play closed testing — the full detail

Written for anyone who wants to know precisely what they are joining, and for our own reference. If you only want the short version, read Testers wanted (Android phone testers) instead.

Why this requirement exists at all

Since November 2023, Google has required personal developer accounts — as opposed to registered organisations — to run a closed test before they can publish an app to everyone. The aim is to stop low-quality and throwaway apps reaching the store, by making a developer demonstrate that real people have used the app on real devices. Beebo Entertainment is a personal account, so the requirement applies to us.

The rule, precisely

  • At least 12 testers must be opted in to the closed test.
  • They must remain continuously opted in for at least 14 days.
  • The 14 days must be unbroken. A tester who opts out and rejoins starts again from day zero, and their earlier time does not count.
  • The count is of testers still opted in at the end — not of everyone who ever joined.

This is why we ask people not to leave early. Losing one tester on day 12 does not cost us two days — it costs us a full fortnight with a replacement.

What Google does not require

There is no rule that testers must open the app daily, and no minimum session count or screen time. Anyone telling you otherwise has confused Google's requirement with advice. What Google does say is that developers should give testers clear instructions and encourage them to use as many features as possible.

That matters because of what comes next.

What happens at the end

Once 12 testers have completed their unbroken 14 days, we apply for production access in the Play Console. That application is a three-part form, and Google reads it:

  • About the closed test — how we recruited testers, which features they used, and how engaged they were.
  • About the app — who it is for, what it is worth to them, and how many installs we expect in the first year.
  • Production readiness — what we changed as a result of tester feedback, and why we believe it is ready.

Google typically reviews within about seven days. Approval is not automatic — the review reads the substance of the answers. This is the real reason we would rather have ten testers who genuinely poked at the app than fifty silent installs: one of the questions we must answer is literally which features they used.

What a tester needs — and what they do not

Beebo is a client for a home media server. It plays the video files already sitting on somebody's own Windows PC, and it carries no catalogue of its own. Taken literally that would mean every tester needed a spare computer, which is an unreasonable thing to ask of a friend doing you a favour.

So the app has a “Look around without a server” mode. Choosing it opens the app properly, with everything that genuinely needs no server working for real — the road-trip games, Campsite Mode, the star chart, the scavenger hunt, the shared packing list, campfire sounds, the badges, and a sample video. The library tabs explain plainly that a library lives on your own computer, rather than showing an error.

A tester with a Windows PC can install the server too and test the whole product. That is more useful to us, but it is not required, and nobody should feel obliged.

What we can and cannot see

  • We see the email address you give us, because Google needs it to grant access to the test.
  • We see the aggregate install and crash figures Google shows every developer in the Play Console.
  • We cannot see your files, your other apps, your location, your contacts, or anything else on your Google account, and we cannot act as you.
  • Beebo itself contains no advertising and no third-party analytics or tracking.

Leaving the test

You can leave whenever you like — there is an opt-out link, and uninstalling is always your choice. We would simply appreciate a message first, so we can recruit a replacement rather than discover the shortfall on day 14.
